## Working papers

**Spatial mismatch and employment in Nigerian cities** [PDF](../assets/pdfs/Nigerian_cities_paper(Njee)_042126.pdf)

<details style="margin-bottom:20px;padding-left: 40px;">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<div align="left" style="padding-left: 20px;line-height:15px">
<p><small>This paper examines whether poor transportation infrastructure, proxied by city shape, impedes access to labor markets in large Nigerian cities. Using novel primary data from thirteen major cities combined with LSMS administrative data, I estimate the effect of city shape on labor force participation, employment type, hours worked, and formal employment, using an instrumental variables where the potential developable footprint derived from 1975 city boundaries, pre-determined historical population growth and natural geographic constraints form an instrument for city shape. I find that more distorted city shapes are associated with higher commuting costs and greater reliance on public transit. IV estimates on labor market outcomes are null in the primary sample but offer suggestive evidence in the larger LSMS sample that workers exit self-employment and the labor market. Heterogeneity analysis reveals important nuances: worse shapes disproportionately reduce women’s formal employment while increasing their labor force participation, with evidence that these gender differences are concentrated in smaller cities. LGA natives respond differently from migrants, with worse shapes shifting their labor market behavior toward that of non-natives. Within-city variation in centrality and neighborhood quality, however, explains little of the observed patterns, suggesting that city-level rather than neighborhood-level spatial organization is the relevant margin.</small></p>
</div>
</details>

**Competition versus biology: Fertility dynamics in polygynous households in Mali** [PDF](../assets/pdfs/Polygamy_fertility_Mali(Njee).pdf)

<details style="margin-bottom:20px;padding-left: 40px;">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<div align="left" style="padding-left: 20px;line-height:15px">
<p><small>I test whether co-wife rivalry drives fertility up in polygynous households in Mali, using three waves of Demographic and Health Surveys (2006-2018) and following the methodology of Rossi (2018) who found strong competition effects in Senegal. Contrary to findings from Senegal, women in polygynous households in Mali have fewer children than those in monogamous marriages, with most of this difference driven by lower-ranked wives. First wives do not significantly reduce birth spacing after the entry of a second wife, showing little evidence of fertility competition between co-wives. Instead, the primary determinants of fertility are biological constraints—particularly age at marriage and length of reproductive periods—which disproportionately limit second wives who marry later. Social constraints, including limited female agency and restrictive gender norms, also appear to prevent women from treating fertility as a strategic choice variable. These findings suggest that in contexts where women lack decision-making power over reproduction, strategic fertility responses to household dynamics may be muted or impossible, with implications for how we model fertility decisions in settings with strong gender inequality.</small></p>
</div>
</details>

## Selected work in progress

**Evaluating Sites-and-Services in Kenya and Tanzania Through Satellite Imagery** with Max Wu

*Draft available upon request*

<details style="margin-bottom:20px;padding-left: 40px;">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<div align="left" style="padding-left: 20px;line-height:15px">
<p><small>I use convolutional neural networks with satellite imagery to evaluate the long-term impacts of World Bank Sites-and-Services housing projects in Kenya and Tanzania, 40-50 years after implementation in the 1960s-1970s. Using a difference-in-differences framework comparing project sites to neighboring wards, I find that Sites-and-Services had minimal impact on wealth: treatment was associated with changes in the asset-based wealth index ranging from 0 to 0.008 points. Strikingly, both treated and control areas experienced large declines in relative economic standing between the early (1978-1982) and later periods (1999-2003), with the small positive effect in Tanzania (0.008) coming entirely from treated areas declining less than control areas rather than from absolute improvements. I also test whether satellite imagery can predict social cohesion outcomes using Afrobarometer data, finding that existing methods cannot reliably predict multi-dimensional social outcomes (R² remains negative across specifications). These results suggest Sites-and-Services may help mitigate economic decline in struggling areas but are not engines of wealth creation, and demonstrate both the promise of satellite-based policy evaluation for concrete outcomes like housing quality and its current limitations for complex social outcomes.</small></p>
</div>
</details>

**What Bounds Fertility in Polygynous Households? Evidence from the Polygyny Belt**

<details style="margin-bottom:20px;padding-left: 40px;">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<div align="left" style="padding-left: 20px;line-height:15px">
<p><small>West and Central Africa combine some of the world's highest fertility rates with highest levels of polygyny, yet surprisingly little is known about how polygynous household structure shapes fertility per wife. Drawing on pooled DHS data focusing on couples from 12 countries in the polygyny belt between 1991 and 2024, I document that wives in polygynous unions have about 0.2 fewer living children than monogamous wives at completed fertility, a gap far narrower than observed at earlier ages and driven almost entirely by junior wives. I propose a biological-constraint mechanism as an alternative to the strategic rivalry explanation emphasized in prior work: as spousal rank rises, the husband's remaining reproductive window tightens, mechanically compressing per-wife fertility. Accounting for the husband's reproductive window substantially shrinks the senior–junior gap, and evidence on sexual activity, where husbands are more likely to have most recently had intercourse with their most junior wife, and activity declines sharply with age, is consistent with this mechanism. These results suggest that fertility decline in West Africa may be slower in part for mechanical reasons tied to marriage-market structure, beyond the bargaining-based channels emphasized in the existing literature.</small></p>
</div>
</details>
